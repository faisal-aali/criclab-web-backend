# What the numbers mean

## Ball speed

The speed of the ball as it leaves the hand, in kilometres per hour. Measured
from the ball's movement across the first part of its flight, corrected for the
fact that the ball is travelling away from the camera as well as across it.

If your clip was filmed in slow motion, the speed shown is the real-world speed,
not the slowed-down one.

## Release point

The exact frame the ball leaves the hand. Everything else — arm speed, the
delivery stride, the phase breakdown — is measured against this moment, so
CricLab spends real effort getting it right rather than estimating it from the
arm position.

## Front foot contact

The frame where the front foot plants and stops moving. The gap between front
foot contact and release is one of the most useful numbers in the report: it
tells you how much of your body is still working for you at the moment the ball
goes.

## Arm speed

How fast the bowling hand is travelling through the delivery swing. Reported in
kilometres per hour so it sits alongside ball speed.

Arm speed is normally a little below ball speed — the ball gains from wrist and
finger action at the last moment. If the two look wrong relative to each other,
CricLab flags it rather than reporting both as if nothing is off.

## Arm rotation

How fast the bowling arm is rotating around the shoulder through the delivery,
in degrees per second.

## Line and length

Where the ball pitched, plotted on a standard pitch map. Length is grouped the
usual way — full toss, yorker, full, good, back of a length, short.

## Elbow extension

The change in elbow angle through the delivery swing. This is the number people
associate with legality, and it is the number a phone camera is least able to
answer. CricLab reports it only when the footage genuinely supports it, and says
plainly when it does not. Treat it as a coaching indicator, never as a ruling.

## Consistency

Bowl several deliveries and CricLab compares them, showing how repeatable your
speed, release point and action are across the set. For most bowlers this is
more valuable than any single delivery.

## Why a number is sometimes missing

A number is left out when the footage cannot support it — the ball was never
clearly visible, the camera angle hid the relevant joint, the light was too poor
to separate the ball from the background, or the frame rate was too low for the
pace being bowled. The report says which of these it was.
